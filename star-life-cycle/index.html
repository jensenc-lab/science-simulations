Vocabulary
Nebula
A vast cloud of gas and dust in space, often the birthplace of new stars. Some nebulae are also formed from the remains of dying stars.
Protostar
A young, contracting clump of gas and dust that has not yet begun nuclear fusion. Once its core gets hot enough, it will become a true star.
Main Sequence
The longest, most stable stage of a star's life, when it fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. About 90% of stars in the universe are in this stage.
Nuclear Fusion
The process in which lighter atomic nuclei combine to form heavier ones, releasing enormous amounts of energy. Fusion in a star's core is what makes it shine.
Red Giant
A late-life stage of a low- to medium-mass star, when its core contracts and its outer layers expand and cool. The star becomes much larger and redder than it was on the main sequence.
Supergiant
A very large, luminous late-life stage of a high-mass star — often hundreds of times the diameter of the Sun. Inside, increasingly heavy elements are forged through fusion.
Planetary Nebula
A glowing shell of gas shed by a dying low- to medium-mass star. The name is misleading: these have nothing to do with planets — early astronomers just thought they looked planet-like through their telescopes.
Supernova
The violent explosion that ends the life of a high-mass star. It scatters newly-forged heavy elements into space and can briefly outshine an entire galaxy.
White Dwarf
The hot, dense remnant core left behind after a low- to medium-mass star sheds its outer layers. About the size of Earth, but with the mass of the Sun.
Neutron Star
An extremely dense remnant left after a supernova, made almost entirely of neutrons. A single teaspoon of neutron-star material would weigh about a billion tons on Earth.
Black Hole
A region of space where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape. Black holes can form when the most massive stars die in supernova explosions.
Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram
A scatter plot that shows the relationship between stars' surface temperatures and their luminosities. Different types of stars cluster in different regions of the diagram.
Stellar Mass
The amount of matter in a star, usually measured in solar masses (M☉, where 1 M☉ = the mass of our Sun). Mass is the single most important factor in determining how a star lives and how it dies.
