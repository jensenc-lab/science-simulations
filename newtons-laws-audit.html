Introduction: Who Was Isaac Newton?
Isaac Newton (1643–1727) was an English mathematician, physicist, and astronomer widely considered one of the most influential scientists of all time. In 1687 he published his landmark work, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, which laid the entire foundation of classical mechanics. His three laws of motion describe how objects respond to forces and have remained the backbone of physics education for more than three centuries. In addition to his laws of motion, Newton also formulated the law of universal gravitation, co-invented calculus, and advanced the study of optics. His work unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics, showing that the same force governing a falling apple also keeps the Moon in orbit.
FUN FACT: Newton reportedly watched an apple fall from a tree, which sparked his thinking about gravity. He also invented calculus AND the reflecting telescope. Overachiever much?
Oh sure, everybody totally understands the concept of inertia the very first time they hear the word. No problem at all. Totally crystal clear. Moving on.
What Are Newton's Three Laws?
Historical Context Newton developed his laws of motion while working at Cambridge University. When the Great Plague of 1665 forced the university to close, Newton retreated to his family estate in Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire. During this period of forced isolation he made his greatest discoveries, including the foundations of calculus, optics, and the laws of motion and gravitation. The three laws were formally published in the Principia Mathematica in 1687 and were immediately recognized as a revolution in scientific thinking.
Forces are the name of the game in physics. Without understanding what forces do, you literally cannot explain anything that moves — or anything that stays still. Newton really hit it out of the park when he came up with these three laws, and they've been a total game-changer for science and engineering ever since.

Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction
Official Statement: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. When object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts a force on object A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
This law is one of the most elegant in all of physics. Every single force in the universe comes in a pair. You cannot push on something without it pushing back on you with exactly the same strength. No exceptions.
When you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on your hand with the same force. When a rocket fires its engines, it pushes exhaust gases downward, and those gases push the rocket upward with equal force — this is how rockets work in the vacuum of space where there is no ground or air to push against.
Everyday Examples:
Swimming: you push water backward with your hands; the water pushes you forward.
A gun firing: the bullet is pushed forward; the gun recoils backward with equal force.
Jumping: you push down on the Earth; the Earth pushes you upward.
A bird flying: wings push air downward; air pushes the bird upward.
Common Misconception
Action-reaction forces act on different objects — they never cancel each other out. The force your foot exerts on the floor and the force the floor exerts on your foot are an action-reaction pair, but they act on different objects (the floor and you). Forces only cancel when they act on the same object.
Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia
Official Statement: An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force.
Objects at rest are basically just chillin' — they have zero desire to start moving on their own. Objects that are already moving? They'll keep on truckin' forever in a straight line unless something acts on them to change that. This stubborn tendency to resist any change in motion is what scientists call inertia. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has, and the harder it is to change its motion.
Think about sitting in a car when it suddenly brakes. Your body lurches forward — that's inertia in action. Your body wanted to keep moving at the car's original speed even though the car stopped. This is precisely why seatbelts exist and why they save lives.
Examples of the First Law:
A soccer ball on a field stays still until a player kicks it.
A hockey puck on ice keeps sliding because friction is very low.
An astronaut in space floats in a straight line at constant speed until a force acts on them.
Tablecloth trick: yank the tablecloth away quickly, and the dishes stay put (their inertia keeps them in place).
Per N1L: when all forces are balanced (net force = 0), there is no change in motion. This is the foundational concept for the AP exam unit on dynamics. NB: do not confuse balanced forces with zero forces — an object can have many forces acting on it and still have zero net force.
Newton's Second Law: Force, Mass & Acceleration
Official Statement: The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. Written as an equation: F = ma
The second law, which is arguably the most mathematically significant of Newton's three laws and which forms the quantitative basis of virtually all dynamics problems you will encounter both in this course and in subsequent advanced coursework, establishes a precise and measurable proportional relationship between the resultant force vector acting upon a body of given inertial mass and the corresponding kinematic quantity of linear acceleration that the body will undergo as a direct consequence of the application of that unbalanced force, such that as the sum of forces increases while holding mass constant the acceleration increases proportionally, and conversely as the mass of the object increases while the total force is held constant the acceleration decreases in an inversely proportional manner, which explains fundamentally why it requires considerably greater force to impart the same acceleration to a more massive object than to a less massive one.
This law is a total game-changer for engineering. It's kind of like hitting a home run in baseball — the harder you swing the bat (more force), the faster the ball accelerates off of it. Or think of it like a quarterback throwing a Hail Mary pass — more force behind the throw means more acceleration of the football.
In F = ma, the "F" represents the net force on the object — that is, all forces combined into one total value. Some textbooks call this the resultant force. Others write it as the total force. Your teacher may also refer to it as the sum of forces. All of these mean the same thing in the formula, but you have to use the combined force, not just one of many forces acting on the object.
Per N2L, F=ma is the single most-used equation on the AP exam. Don't confuse it with N3L. Remember: F is always the net force (not any individual force), m is measured in kg, and a is in m/s². Master this and you're golden.
Examples of the Second Law:
Pushing a shopping cart: applying more force produces more acceleration; a heavier cart (more mass) produces less acceleration for the same push.
A rocket burning more fuel produces greater thrust force, causing greater acceleration.
A tennis ball and bowling ball hit with the same force: the tennis ball accelerates much more because it has far less mass.
Braking a car: the braking force causes deceleration (negative acceleration); a heavier truck requires more braking force to stop in the same distance as a lighter car.

Deeper Dive: The Mathematics Behind the Laws
Newton's second law can be expressed more precisely using calculus as F = dp/dt, where p represents momentum (defined as mass times velocity, p = mv). For objects with constant mass this simplifies to F = m(dv/dt) = ma, which is the form used in introductory physics. The first law is mathematically a special case of the second: when net force F = 0, then ma = 0, and since mass m must be positive, acceleration a must equal zero — meaning constant velocity, which includes the special case of zero velocity (rest). The third law can be derived from the principle of conservation of momentum: in an isolated system with no external forces, total momentum is conserved. If object A exerts force F_AB on object B, then by conservation of momentum, object B must exert force F_BA on object A such that F_AB = −F_BA. Newton expressed this as his third law. Together, the three laws of motion plus Newton's law of universal gravitation (F = Gm₁m₂/r²) form the complete framework of classical mechanics, accurate for objects moving at speeds much less than the speed of light. At relativistic speeds Einstein's special relativity supersedes Newton's framework, and at quantum scales (subatomic particles) quantum mechanics applies instead. For all practical engineering problems at human scales, however, Newtonian mechanics remains fully accurate and in universal use today.
Newton's Lasting Legacy
Isaac Newton's three laws of motion transformed humanity's understanding of the physical world and ignited the Scientific Revolution. Published in his 1687 masterwork, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, these laws gave scientists and engineers a precise mathematical framework for predicting the motion of any object under any force. For over two centuries, Newtonian mechanics was considered the complete and final description of the physical universe. Even today, with the advent of quantum mechanics and general relativity, Newton's laws remain the standard toolkit for engineers, architects, and physicists working at human scales. Satellites are placed in orbit, bridges are built, aircraft are designed, and spacecraft are navigated to distant planets — all using principles that Newton first described in a farmhouse in Lincolnshire over 330 years ago. Beyond their practical applications, Newton's laws represent one of the greatest intellectual achievements in human history: the discovery that the universe obeys precise, universal, mathematical rules.
